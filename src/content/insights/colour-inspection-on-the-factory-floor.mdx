Ask a quality manager about automated colour inspection and you will often hear the same story: it worked during commissioning, then slowly started rejecting good parts&nbsp;— or worse, passing bad ones.

The model rarely changed. The *light* did.

## The camera does not see colour, it sees light

A colour camera records the light reflected from a part, shaped by the light source, the lens, the sensor and its automatic settings. Change any of these and the recorded colour changes, even though the part has not.

Common culprits on a real line:

- Daylight through skylights changing over the day
- Fluorescent tubes ageing or being replaced with LEDs
- Auto-exposure and auto-white-balance "helpfully" correcting each frame
- Dust on the lens or the lighting cover

## A pipeline that holds its calibration

**Enclose and control the light.** A hood or tunnel with fixed, daylight-balanced (D65) LEDs removes ambient light from the equation.

**Lock the camera.** Exposure, gain and white balance are fixed. Nothing on the camera is allowed to adapt.

**Calibrate every shift.** The station images a colour reference card at shift start and computes a correction. If the correction drifts beyond a limit, the station raises a maintenance flag instead of silently compensating.

**Measure in a perceptual space.** Pixels are converted to CIELAB and compared with the approved master sample using **ΔE2000**, which tracks how differences are perceived by people better than simple RGB distances. The customer sets the tolerance&nbsp;— for example ΔE ≤ 1.5.

**Look at patterns, not just averages.** Average colour can be in tolerance while a streak, blotch or uneven coating ruins the part. An anomaly model trained on good parts runs alongside the colour measurement.

**Alarm on the trend.** A slow drift toward the limit is often the most useful signal of all&nbsp;— it points to a dosing, temperature or supplier change upstream before any part fails.

## Be honest about what a camera can do

An industrial RGB camera under controlled light measures colour *difference* against a master sample very well. It is not a laboratory spectrophotometer. When a specification demands lab-grade absolute colour, we integrate the customer's spectrophotometer or use a multispectral camera&nbsp;— and we say so in the feasibility report.
